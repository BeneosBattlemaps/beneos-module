Before you read: the landing page works out of the box. Set it active, invite your players, and it runs. Everything below is for the GM who wants to drive it well, or bend it to a campaign of their own. It is built for hell-themed play and pairs with adventures like Descent into Avernus, but nothing stops you using it for any infernal story.

The Avernus landing page is not one scene. It is a small set of them, wired together into a hub your players can walk around on their own: a home page, a headquarters table, a page of faces they have met, a hell world map, and a garage for the war machines they capture. You reveal each part as the campaign earns it.

Landing page & navigation

This is the first thing your players see when they log in, so keep it active by default at the start of a session. From here they reach every scene you have unlocked for them. At the beginning only the Player Table and the Characters page are open; you unlock the rest, the world map, the garage, as the story reaches them.

Session title

Give each session an atmospheric title on the landing page: a session number and a small ominous hint of what is coming. It sets the mood before a word is spoken. Grab the Drawing Tools from the left toolbar and double-click the title text to edit it. If it does not suit your story, hide the title and its bar entirely.

How the navigation works

Each button is two tiles: an invisible clickable tile sitting over a visible text label, run by Monks Active Tile Triggers. To open a navigation point for players: pick Tile Controls, nudge the invisible tile aside, right-click the text tile beneath and Toggle Visibility State to show it, slide the invisible tile back over the label and lock it, then right-click the invisible tile and toggle its active state on. Do the same on the other pages so navigation stays seamless wherever the players are.

If MATT is ever between updates

Monks Active Tile Triggers is often one of the last modules to catch up after a big Foundry version jump. For those short windows the pages ship with a backup navigation made of ordinary journal icons, the same kind used elsewhere on Beneos maps. Grant your players ownership of those journals and drag the icons onto the bar, and they can right-click to travel until MATT is current again. Ninety-nine times in a hundred you can ignore it.

Background music

Every scene here carries its own sound source that starts when you enter it. Scenes that share a track play on without a gap as you move between them. To change or silence it, right-click the scene, open Configure, and under the Ambience tab clear or replace the Scene Playlist. See Ambience & Sound for more.

The Player Table

The Player Table is the group's headquarters, everything they need gathered on one surface. Read it as a base in a cave, a dungeon, wherever your story wants. If you are not running the official module, switch off the animated logo in the top corner and drop in your own.

Infernal contracts

When a player signs a devil's bargain, reveal that contract on the table by right-clicking its image and making it visible. Write the actual terms into a journal, share it, and drag it onto the visible contract so players can read their own deals whenever they like.

Soul coins

Next to the contracts is a small chest for tracking soul coins. Hide the tile covering it to open the chest for your players, then show or hide individual coins as their hoard rises and falls. A little counter below reads out the total at a glance, up to nineteen.

The quest log

The large book on the table is the group's shared quest log. Type quest titles with the text tool, tick the checkbox tiles as jobs are done, or mark a finished one with an X. The book takes handouts and images too, so it doubles as a pinboard for anything the party needs to remember.

Player sheets

Bottom right holds a row of player sheets, six set up for a group of six. Activate one per player, rename the placeholders to your characters, then link each sheet's hidden token to the right character through Open Configuration and Represented Actor. After that a player can double-click their sheet to open their own character, check inventory and spells, or adjust HP straight from the table. Need more than six? Copy a sheet and set it up the same way.

The Characters page

Campaigns pile up faces fast. This page is where your players keep track of who they have met and where. The characters are grouped by location across three sub-pages, so there is room for a whole campaign's cast.

Every portrait is a real actor from your Actor directory. To reveal one, pick Token Controls, click the character's token, then right-click and Toggle Visibility State. Players never see the stat block, but once a face is visible they can read the name by hovering over it. To add your own NPC, drag the actor onto the scene, open its token configuration, and set Display Name to "Hovered by Anyone" so players can read the name once you show it.

The hell world map

Do not let this scene overwhelm you. It carries a lot of GM machinery, but your players only ever see the landscape and their own token. Use as much or as little as your group wants; a one-shot can ignore every marker and still get a good map.

A hexcrawl through hell

The map is sixty hexes, labelled A1 to J6, each a biome of its own: desert, burning plains, lava fields. Every hex holds at least one point of interest marked by a journal icon you double-click to read. Even with no POIs touched, the grid alone runs as a survival crawl across a hellish waste. It is built on the structure popularised by The Alexandrian, extended with third-party and homebrew content, and every outside source is linked inside its own POI.

Reading the POI colours

The point-of-interest icons are colour-coded so you know at a glance where each one comes from: blue for official Descent into Avernus content, purple for free third-party material from The Alexandrian, and yellow for extra third-party content you can buy elsewhere, with links and authors named in each journal.

Revealing a location to players

When the party discovers a place, grant them the journal: pick Journal Notes, click the icon on the hex, find its title in the Journal tab, right-click, Configure Ownership, and set it to All Players Observer. Now they can open and explore it on their own. Fog of War keeps the rest hidden until their token gets there.

Two ways to play it

You can run everything on this one global map, which is plenty for most groups, or treat the six lettered regions (A to F) as six rooms of a dungeon and drop into a detailed regional map for each. More on that just below.

A simpler map, and future growth

If the detailed grid is too busy there is a stripped-down version with fewer POIs you can switch to under the tiles. And the map grows: greyed-out icons are locations still to come, and installing the matching release lights them up automatically. Hover an icon to see which release it belongs to.

Regional maps

Regional maps are close-ups of single areas from the global map, each rendered at full quality so every point of interest reads clearly, like a level in a video game. The hex layout matches the global map exactly, so the connections are the same; only the detail changes. Your players move hex to hex as a group and can leave one region to enter the next.

At a region's edge sit navigation hexes marked by an arrow and a journal icon. A token standing there can travel the way the arrow points, and you trigger the move by clicking the journal's eye. A minimap in the top-left corner shows all the regions at a glance. On regional maps the POIs are listed down the right-hand side rather than sitting on the hexes, which stops an accidental click from teleporting anyone. Each region also has its own tilt-shift scenery, the world shown as a little diorama, if that adds to your table's immersion.

The Garage

The garage is where the party shows off the infernal war machines they have taken. It starts empty, just an animated background, and fills up as your players earn vehicles. Five are provided; hand them out one at a time, or park your own designs here instead.

To reveal a vehicle, pick Tile Controls, use the Select Drawings tool to click the machine, then right-click and Toggle Visibility State. Do the same for its name label with the drawing tool so the plate appears beside it.

A GM trick: fold the garage, the table, and the other back rooms into the story as a pocket dimension the party can step into, a mobile base for negotiations, storage, or showing off their fleet. To stop it becoming a free safe haven, give it teeth: the place was built for devils, so it is brutally hot, and an hour inside costs a human a level of exhaustion.
